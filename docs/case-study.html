Reliable but Invalid

I built an 840-item benchmark for Chinese web fiction, validated it against real reader behaviour, and found that the two standard warrants for LLM-as-judge evaluation— test–retest reliability and inter-judge agreement—are structurally incapable of detecting an invalid rubric.

Why this matters

If you run model evals, this is your failure mode too

Teams justify LLM judges two ways: the judge is consistent with itself, and several judges agree with each other. Both held in my data. Validity did not.

The judge scored 0.87 on reliability and 0.14 on validity

Test–retest ICC(1,1) = .874 across 177 texts scored three times each. Four judges from three vendors correlated .45–.49 with one another. None exceeded r = .14 against any measure of what readers actually did. Disattenuation moved the estimate by .002—noise was never the constraint.

Consistency measures agreement, not correctness. Consensus only cancels error when errors are independent, and here they were shared.

And agreement statistics hide the disagreement that matters

Inter-rater agreement is normally reported as correlation, which is invariant to level shifts. On the same 21 novels one judge averaged 5.65 and another 8.00. Two judges can correlate at .9 on rankings and still return opposite verdicts on the comparison you actually care about—because that verdict depends on calibration, not rank order.

Evidence

Four judges, one set of texts, opposite conclusions

What I built

The pipeline

DATA

A living corpus with an external criterion

1,110 serialized novels with platform reader ratings embedded in each file, refreshed on a daily incremental job. Behavioural metadata for 797 titles pulled through the platform API at 96.6% success, with rate limiting, a circuit breaker, incremental checkpointing and resume. The 5.1% that could not be retrieved were tested and found non-random (Cohen's d = −0.98 on rating) and reported as such.

EVAL

Contamination control computed, not assumed

Exclusions were derived per cell as the union of every text the pipeline actually touched—rubric induction, reviewer calibration, and the generator's own prompt. An audit of that contact surface found a leak I had missed in one cell. Correcting it moved the headline estimate from .136 to .138.

CRITERION

Six reader-response measures, not one

Platform ratings are contaminated—length and exposure jointly explain a median 27.9% of their variance. Rather than defend a single clean criterion, I used six with differing contamination profiles. Validity rose monotonically as the criterion got cleaner (.081 for raw rating → .138 for exposure-normalized bookmark rate), which is itself evidence the weak signal is real.

ANALYSIS

Within-cell standardization and stated power

All correlations pooled after within-cell z-scoring. Every estimate is reported against the minimum detectable effect for its n, so a null is labelled precise or underpowered rather than left ambiguous. Range restriction corrected via Thorndike Case II; multiplicity handled by permutation and FDR.

Diagnosis

The defect was in composition, not content

Craft dimensions carried signal; originality dimensions carried almost none. No dimension was negative—so nothing was actively wrong. Averaging uninformative dimensions into the composite simply diluted it, leaving the total weaker than its strongest component. Principal components showed the ten dimensions collapsing onto two factors (PC1 = 54.5%).

Multidimensional rubrics are not free

A ten-dimension rubric looks more thorough than a one-dimension one. Here it was measurably worse, and it had roughly two dimensions of real content behind ten labels. That is a concrete, testable claim about rubric design, not a stylistic preference.

Limits

What I would tell a reviewer before they ask

  • The criterion is behavioural, not aesthetic. I measure what readers did— bookmarked, kept reading—not what they judged to be good. The construct is scoped to reader response as manifested in platform behaviour, and the word "quality" appears nowhere in the conclusions.
  • One platform, one language, one genre family. Serialized web fiction is highly formulaic, which makes it a favourable test bed rather than a representative one.
  • Missing data is non-random. The 5.1% unavailable through platform search skew low-rated, truncating the sample at the bottom. This attenuates correlations, so the reported estimates are likely conservative.
  • The rubric was human-vetted, which strengthens the result. Dimensions came from researcher-specified seeds, model adaptation per genre, then researcher review. Expert vetting did not rescue validity—a researcher-authored fixed rubric performed equivalently.

An errata log ships with the repository

Six defects in my own analysis code were found and corrected during the study, three of which changed reported numbers. They are documented rather than quietly fixed, because in work like this the half-life of an intermediate conclusion is short and the errors hide inside figures that already look confirmed.
